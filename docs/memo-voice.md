# Memo Voice & Style Guide

**Purpose:** Style and voice guidance for the 1-page institutional investment memo (Part 1 deliverable). The design system is locked ("Institutional Classic" — see [deck-structure.md](deck-structure.md)). This document covers *how to write* inside that design.

**Anchor reference:** Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters (Warren Buffett, 1977 / 1989 / 1991 / 2001 / 2007). Supporting exemplars: Howard Marks (Oaktree) memos *Sea Change* (2022) and *On Bubble Watch* (2025); David Einhorn (Greenlight) quarterly position writeups; Trian / Pershing Square activist white papers.

**How to use this doc:** Read top-to-bottom once before drafting. While drafting, keep the *Plain-English Vocabulary*, *Sourcing Patterns*, and the *Annotated Memo Skeleton* sections open in a second pane. After a draft is done, run the *Voice Principles* as a checklist.

---

## 1. Voice Principles (the eight rules)

Each rule is stated once, then shown with a real sentence from an exemplar so you can hear it.

### 1.1 Lead with the number, then the meaning.
The first sentence of a section should state a fact the reader can grade you on. Interpretation comes second.

> "Berkshire's *loss* in net worth during 2001 was $3.77 billion, which decreased the per-share book value of both our Class A and Class B stock by 6.2%. Though our corporate performance last year was satisfactory, *my* performance was anything but." — Buffett, 2001 letter

Notice: number, then judgment. The judgment is short, plain, and personal.

### 1.2 Name the manager, the date, and the source — in the sentence.
Provenance lives inside the prose, not in a footnote.

> "Twenty years ago, on January 3, 1972, Blue Chip Stamps (then an affiliate of Berkshire and later merged into it) bought control of See's Candy Shops, a West Coast manufacturer and retailer of boxed-chocolates." — Buffett, 1991 letter

Notice: exact date, exact entity, exact corporate plumbing — all in one sentence, all readable.

### 1.3 Translate every term of art the first time it appears.
If a teenager could not follow the sentence, rewrite it.

> "Float is money we hold but don't own. In an insurance operation, float arises because premiums are received before losses are paid, an interval that sometimes extends over many years." — Buffett, 2001 letter

> "The combined ratio represents total insurance costs (losses incurred plus expenses) compared to revenue from premiums: A ratio below 100 indicates an underwriting profit, and one above 100 indicates a loss." — Buffett, 1989 letter

Notice: both sentences could appear in a high-school textbook. Neither loses precision.

### 1.4 Use one analogy where a paragraph of theory would otherwise be needed.
Pick a concrete, physical, non-financial image. Use it once. Move on.

> "In economic terms, the liability resembles an interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury that comes due only at our election." — Buffett, 1989 letter (on deferred tax)

> "I violated the Noah rule: Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does." — Buffett, 2001 letter (on risk underwriting)

Notice: the image carries the whole point. No academic restatement follows.

### 1.5 Stage tension between what was said and what actually happened.
The most persuasive sentences in a memo are the ones that put two facts next to each other and force the reader to notice the gap.

> "We have mistakenly predicted better results in each of the last two years." — Buffett, 1977 letter

> "In setting prices and also in evaluating aggregation risk, we had either overlooked or dismissed the possibility of large-scale terrorism losses. That was a relevant underwriting factor, and we ignored it." — Buffett, 2001 letter (on General Re)

Notice: the prior position is named (we predicted; we evaluated). The new fact lands on top of it.

### 1.6 Show how the narrative changed, and date the change.
Investment cases live in time. Write them in time.

> "Back when Berkshire's book value was $19.46, intrinsic value was somewhat less because the book value was entirely tied up in a textile business not worth the figure at which it was carried. Now most of our businesses are worth far more than their carrying values." — Buffett, 1989 letter

> "I continued to note these qualities for the next 52 years as Coke blanketed the world. During this period, however, I carefully avoided buying even a single share. Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes." — Buffett, 1989 letter

Notice: a "then / now" sentence pair. The pivot date is explicit.

### 1.7 Forward views are *conditional*, not predicted.
Say what would have to be true, then say whether you believe it.

> "If no mega-catastrophe occurs, I — once again — expect the cost of our float to be low in the coming year. We will indeed need a low cost, as will *all* insurers." — Buffett, 2001 letter

> "We never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are." — Marks, *Sea Change* (Dec 2022)

Notice: "if X, then Y." The conditional is the honesty.

### 1.8 Close with restraint, not a sales line.
A memo that ends with "this is a compelling opportunity" is a memo nobody quotes back to you. End on a sentence that could be tested against reality.

> "We need 'elephants' to make significant gains now — and they are hard to find." — Buffett, 2001 letter

> "Thus, I can't speak authoritatively about whether we're in a bubble. I just want to lay out the facts as I see them and suggest how you might think about them." — Marks, *On Bubble Watch* (Jan 2025)

Notice: the writer hands judgment to the reader. The reader does not feel sold.

## 2. Structural Patterns

How good memos open, build, cite, and close. These are patterns you can copy directly.

### 2.1 How they open
The strongest openings do *one* of three things, and never more than one:

1. **State a result, then judge it.** Buffett's standard move. "X happened. Here is what to make of X."
   > "Operating earnings in 1977 of $21,904,000, or $22.54 per share, were moderately better than anticipated a year ago." — 1977 letter

2. **Claim a regime change.** Marks's standard move. "Most of the time the world is stable; today is different."
   > "In my 53 years in the investment world, I've seen a number of economic cycles, pendulum swings, manias and panics, bubbles and crashes, but I remember only two real sea changes. I think we may be in the midst of a third one today." — *Sea Change*

3. **Anchor in a date and a transaction.** Activist / acquisition-style. "On date X, entity Y did Z."
   > "Twenty years ago, on January 3, 1972, Blue Chip Stamps... bought control of See's Candy Shops..." — 1991 letter

For our 1-page memo, default to #1 (state a number from filings, then the judgment). #2 only works if the market thesis is the lead. Avoid generic scene-setting ("In today's volatile environment...").

### 2.2 How they introduce a specific company
The pattern is: **what the company *is*, what it actually *does* day-to-day, and who runs it.** Three sentences max.

> "It was early in 1967 that we made our entry into this industry through the purchase of National Indemnity Company and National Fire and Marine Insurance Company (sister companies) for approximately $8.6 million." — Buffett, 1977

> "GEICO, by far our largest primary insurer, made major progress in 2001, thanks to Tony Nicely, its CEO, and his associates. Quite simply, Tony is an owner's dream." — Buffett, 2001

Note the structural unit: *role in the portfolio / what they sell / who runs them*. For our memo: "$TICKER is the [position in market] supplier of [product] to [end customer]. The CEO, [Name], has run the business since [date]." That is the introduction. Done in one sentence.

### 2.3 How they build the bull / bear tension
The dominant pattern across Buffett, Marks, and Einhorn is **"market sees X. We see Y because Z."** stated explicitly, not implied. Marks calls it the gap between sentiment and reality:

> "There's a grain of truth that underlies every mania and bubble. It just gets taken too far." — Marks, *On Bubble Watch*

> "When people assume — and price in — an expectation that things can only get better, the damage done by negative surprises is profound." — Marks, *On Bubble Watch*

Einhorn does the same with sharper edges by quoting management directly and putting the quote in tension with a fact:

> "When the CEO was asked on the April 27th quarterly call what the plans were for playing his 'really strong hand of cards here,' he responded with, 'I'd like to buy the whole company back myself.'" — Einhorn, Greenlight TWTR writeup

The construction is always: (a) consensus or management view in their own words, (b) the fact that contradicts it, (c) the implication. Three beats.

### 2.4 How they cite sources inline (no footnotes required)
The pattern is **prepositional phrase + named source**. The source becomes part of the sentence's grammar.

- "In the 1985 letter to shareholders, the CEO said..." (Buffett style)
- "On the Q1 2024 earnings call, the CFO acknowledged..." (Einhorn style)
- "The 10-K filed February 2025 discloses..." (filings style)
- "As I wrote in the memo *On the Couch* (January 2016)..." (Marks self-citation)

This means each claim's provenance is testable without breaking the reader's flow. A reader can audit the memo by asking "did the CFO actually say that on Q1?" — and the memo tells them where to look.

Marks's self-citation pattern is particularly useful when a memo references the writer's prior work:

> "As I wrote in the memo *On the Couch* (January 2016), whereas events in the real world fluctuate between 'pretty good' and 'not so hot,' investor sentiment often careens from 'flawless' to 'hopeless'..." — *Sea Change*

Note: italic memo title, parenthetical date, comma, paraphrase. Steal this exactly.

### 2.5 How they handle risks
Risks in great memos are not a bulleted appendix. They are *the same kind of sentence* as the thesis — a conditional ("if X happens, our Y is wrong"), with a number attached.

> "Unless rates rise at a comparable 1% per month, underwriting profits must shrink. Recently the pace of rate increases has slowed dramatically." — Buffett, 1977 letter

The verb "must" plus a measurable threshold ("1% per month") is the move. Bad risk writing says "execution risk is high." Good risk writing says "if same-store sales growth drops below 2%, the refinance window in 2027 closes and the bond covenant trips."

### 2.6 How they close
The two best closings:

1. **A condition, not a conclusion.** "If A, B, and C happen — and we believe they will, because D — the business is worth E."

2. **A handoff to the reader.** "Here is what I see. Decide for yourself." (Marks)

> "Charlie and I will strive for above-average performance and will not be satisfied with less. But two conditions at Berkshire are far different from what they once were." — Buffett, 2001 letter

Resist any sentence that contains "compelling," "attractive risk-reward," or "asymmetric upside." Those are sell-side phrases. The institutional reader recognizes them as filler.

## 4. Sourcing Patterns

How to make every claim auditable without breaking the line.

### 4.1 The prepositional phrase
Source lives at the front of the sentence, as a prepositional phrase.

- "In the FY2024 10-K (filed February 2025), the company discloses that..."
- "On the Q3 2025 earnings call, the CFO said..."
- "In the May 8, 2024 investor day deck, slide 23 shows..."

This is the dominant pattern in activist white papers (Trian, Pershing) and works perfectly for our memo because it tells the reader exactly which document to open if they want to test the claim.

### 4.2 The named-speaker quote
When a claim turns on what management said, *quote them*, name them, date the quote.

> "When the CEO was asked on the April 27th quarterly call what the plans were for playing his 'really strong hand of cards here,' he responded with, 'I'd like to buy the whole company back myself.'" — Einhorn

Pattern: speaker title + date of utterance + venue + direct quotation + (often) the contradicting fact in the next sentence.

### 4.3 The self-citation
For our memo, this matters less, but if we reference the broader Lunchline framework or a prior workstream, use Marks's pattern: italic title + parenthetical date.

> "As I wrote in the memo *On the Couch* (January 2016), ..."

### 4.4 The peer cross-reference
When making a claim about how the *industry* behaves, cite a peer transcript or filing — not a "sector study."

- "Two of [TICKER]'s three largest peers — [PEER A] (Q2 2025 call) and [PEER B] (FY2024 10-K) — have raised list prices [X%] over the same period. [TICKER] has not."

This is how a memo demonstrates that the writer has actually read the peer set rather than parroting a sector report.

### 4.5 What *not* to do
- "Company filings" (no specific document)
- "Public reports" (no source)
- "Industry sources" (no source)
- "Multiple analysts" (which analysts? in which notes?)
- Any sentence with a number and no preposition pointing to where the number came from.

The discipline: if the reader can't find the source in under sixty seconds using only the memo's text, the source isn't cited.

## 5. Narrative Arc Templates

Three opening patterns, each with the actual exemplar sentence(s) underneath. Pick one per memo. Do not mix.

### Template A — "Market sees X. We see Y."

The Steinhardt / VIC house style. State the consensus position first, in the consensus's own language. Then state the variant view in one sentence. Then the *because*.

Exemplar (Buffett doing it about himself, retroactively):
> "I continued to note these qualities for the next 52 years as Coke blanketed the world. During this period, however, I carefully avoided buying even a single share. Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes." — Buffett, 1989

Adapted for our memo:
> "Sell-side coverage of [TICKER] frames the business as a [consensus label] facing [consensus headwind], and prices it at [X]x EBITDA — a discount to peers at [Y]x. We see the same numbers and reach the opposite conclusion: [variant claim]. The reason is [specific fact from filings or transcripts that the consensus has not absorbed]."

### Template B — "The business changed in [year] when..."

The inflection-point opener. Best when there is a discrete event — new CEO, divestiture, regulation, contract change — that the market has not fully repriced.

Exemplar:
> "Back when Berkshire's book value was $19.46, intrinsic value was somewhat less because the book value was entirely tied up in a textile business not worth the figure at which it was carried. Now most of our businesses are worth far more than their carrying values." — Buffett, 1989

Adapted for our memo:
> "Until [year], [TICKER] was a [old description]. In [month year], [specific event — divestiture / new CEO / regulation / lost customer]. The financial statements still describe the old business, but [specific filing or call] discloses that [evidence of the new business]. Twelve months from now, the GAAP earnings will catch up to that disclosure."

### Template C — "Regime change makes [TICKER] structurally different."

Marks's *Sea Change* opener, applied at the single-name level. Use when the *market environment* — not the company — is the variant perception.

Exemplar:
> "In my 53 years in the investment world, I've seen a number of economic cycles, pendulum swings, manias and panics, bubbles and crashes, but I remember only two real sea changes. I think we may be in the midst of a third one today." — Marks, *Sea Change*

Adapted for our memo:
> "For the last [decade / decade-plus], [TICKER]'s end market behaved as a [old characterization, e.g., 'low-growth commodity business priced on capacity utilization']. Three changes since [date] — [change 1], [change 2], [change 3] — have made that characterization wrong. The stock still trades on the old characterization."

**Selection rule:** A goes with company-specific mispricing. B goes with a discrete inflection. C goes with a market-environment thesis. The case brief asks for both a company thesis *and* a market thesis — the memo should have *one dominant* arc (pick A, B, or C) and the other arc as a supporting paragraph.

## 6. Annotated Exemplar Excerpts

Three short passages, each followed by parenthetical marginalia explaining the move. Read these last, write your draft, then re-read these.

### Excerpt 1 — Buffett on a mistake (2001 letter, on General Re)

> "Though our corporate performance last year was satisfactory, *my* performance was anything but. I allowed General Re to take on business without a safeguard I knew was important, and on September 11th, this error caught up with us. ... In setting prices and also in evaluating aggregation risk, we had either overlooked or dismissed the possibility of large-scale terrorism losses. That was a relevant underwriting factor, and we ignored it."

*(Why it works: First-person ownership of the error ("my performance"); names the specific business unit (General Re); names the specific date (September 11); names the specific factor that was missed (aggregation risk for large-scale terrorism). Four nouns and one verb of failure. No adjectives, no hedging, no "in retrospect." This is the gold standard for the "Risks" section of our memo: name the thing that has to go right, name the specific mechanism, take responsibility for the claim.)*

### Excerpt 2 — Buffett opening a position (1989 letter, on Coca-Cola)

> "I continued to note these qualities for the next 52 years as Coke blanketed the world. During this period, however, I carefully avoided buying even a single share. Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes. ... Through a truly rare blend of marketing and financial skills, Roberto has maximized both the growth of his product and the rewards that this growth brings to shareholders."

*(Why it works: The narrative arc is "I was wrong for 52 years; then in summer 1988, I changed my mind." It dates the pivot. It names the operator ("Roberto" — Goizueta, not "management"). It states the operator's two specific competencies (marketing + financial), which makes the thesis testable: if Goizueta leaves, the thesis weakens. The phrase "my brain finally established contact with my eyes" is plain enough for any reader, and the self-deprecation buys credibility for the bull case that follows.)*

### Excerpt 3 — Marks on regime change (*Sea Change*, Dec 2022)

> "In my 53 years in the investment world, I've seen a number of economic cycles, pendulum swings, manias and panics, bubbles and crashes, but I remember only two real sea changes. I think we may be in the midst of a third one today. ... We've gone from the low-return world of 2009-21 to a full-return world, and it may become more so in the near term. ... We never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are."

*(Why it works: Opens with a credential ("53 years") not as bragging but as the basis for the rarity claim ("only two real sea changes"). Dates both regimes ("2009-21" → today). Closes with a conditional that is humble about prediction but firm about diagnosis. This is the template for the market-environment paragraph in our memo: name the prior regime by years, name the new regime by years, name the bridge.)*

### Excerpt 4 — Einhorn on management vs. fact (Greenlight, TWTR writeup)

> "We purchased a position in TWTR at an average of $37.24 per share. ... At this price there is $17 per share of upside if TWTR prevails in court and we believe about $17 per share of downside, if the deal breaks. So, we are getting 50-50 odds on something that should happen 95%+ of the time. ... When the CEO was asked on the April 27th quarterly call what the plans were for playing his 'really strong hand of cards here,' he responded with, 'I'd like to buy the whole company back myself.'"

*(Why it works: Specific entry price ($37.24, not "in the high 30s"); specific upside and downside in dollars per share, not percentages; dated quote from the CEO (April 27th call) used to construct the tension. The sardonic edge ("50-50 odds on something that should happen 95%+ of the time") is earned because the previous two sentences laid the arithmetic. For our memo, this is the model for the *Why Mispriced* paragraph: state the math, then quote management, then let the reader notice the gap.)*

### Excerpt 5 — Buffett's acquisition criteria (1991 letter, on what they look for)

> "(1) Large purchases (at least $10 million of after-tax earnings), (2) Demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are 'turnaround' situations), (3) Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt, (4) Management in place (we can't supply it), (5) Simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it), (6) An offering price."

*(Why it works: Six criteria, each one sentence, each one *operationally testable*. "Demonstrated consistent earning power" is followed by the parenthetical that explicitly rules out turnarounds — a single phrase that does more work than a page of philosophy. For our "What Would Need to Be True" closing, this is the structural template: a numbered list where every item is something the reader can verify against the filings.)*

## 7. Annotated Memo Skeleton

A section-by-section skeleton for the 1-page memo. Each section gives the *purpose*, the *target sentence count*, and *one example sentence* in the voice. Replace the bracketed placeholders.

This skeleton assumes Template A ("market sees X / we see Y") as the dominant arc. Notes at the end show how to swap in Template B or C.

---

### [Headline — single sentence, top of page]

*Purpose:* The thesis, stated as a falsifiable claim with a number.

*Example:* "[TICKER] trades at [X]x [year+1] EBITDA on consensus that assumes [explicit consensus assumption]; we believe [year+1] EBITDA will print [Y]% above consensus because [one-clause reason], implying a fair value of $[Z] at peer-average [W]x — [P]% above today's $[current price]."

*Voice check:* number, then judgment. No adjectives. Reader knows in one sentence what would have to happen for the writer to be wrong.

---

### Section 1 — The Business (2-3 sentences)

*Purpose:* What the company is, what it actually does day-to-day, who runs it. No history.

*Example:*
> "[TICKER] is the [#1 / #2] North American [supplier / operator] of [product or service] to [end customer], generating $[X] of FY2024 revenue (FY2024 10-K, filed [date]) at [Y]% EBITDA margins. The CEO, [Name], has run the business since [year]; before that, [one-line credential]. The single most useful fact about how the business makes money is [unit economic — e.g., 'each install carries 70% gross margin and pays back in 14 months,' with source]."

*Voice check:* one sentence with the topline number and its source; one sentence on the operator; one sentence that earns the rest of the memo by stating a unit economic the reader could verify.

---

### Section 2 — What the Market Sees (2-3 sentences)

*Purpose:* Steelman the consensus in the consensus's own language. Quote a sell-side note or earnings-call analyst question if possible.

*Example:*
> "Sell-side coverage frames [TICKER] as a [consensus label, e.g., 'cyclical industrial exposed to a softening end market']. On the Q[N] [year] earnings call, the lead analyst at [bank] asked, '[paraphrase of skeptical question],' and the CFO acknowledged, '[direct quote].' The result: [TICKER] trades at [X]x forward EBITDA against peers at [Y]x — a discount of [Z]%."

*Voice check:* the consensus is stated as if the writer agrees with it. The discount is quantified. The reader believes the writer has read the bear case.

---

### Section 3 — What We See (3-4 sentences)

*Purpose:* The variant view, in two beats: (a) the fact the consensus has missed; (b) the implication.

*Example:*
> "The consensus framing misses a change disclosed in the [date] 10-K but not yet reflected in coverage: [specific disclosure — e.g., a new long-term contract, a pricing reset, a capacity shift, a regulatory clarification]. Management has so far described this change in [understated language]; the underlying economics imply [quantified impact — e.g., '$[N] of incremental EBITDA at full run-rate, against the consensus [year+1] estimate of $[M]']. Two independent confirmations support the magnitude: [PEER A] reported a comparable shift in its Q[N] [year] call, and [supply-chain or customer evidence with source]. Net: by [year+2], the reported financial statements will look like a different business than the one the current multiple is pricing."

*Voice check:* every claim has a preposition pointing to a source. The variant view rests on a disclosure (testable), a peer cross-reference (testable), and a third-party data point (testable). No "we believe" statements floating without an anchor.

---

### Section 4 — Why It's Mispriced (2-3 sentences)

*Purpose:* The Lunchline-specific section. Name the reason a fact in the filings hasn't been absorbed by the price.

*Example:*
> "Three structural reasons the disclosure has not been priced: (1) [TICKER] sits below the coverage threshold of the [N] funds for which the position would be too small at <[X]% of AUM; (2) the [date] 10-K buries the relevant disclosure on page [N] under the [section name]; (3) management has consistently underdescribed the change on calls, presumably to manage customer expectations — for example, on the Q[N] call the CEO described [the change] as '[modest direct quote],' which the audited footnote on page [N] of the 10-K then quantifies at [a much larger number]."

*Voice check:* exactly the move from Einhorn — quote the management understatement, then quote the filing that contradicts it. The mispricing reason is operational (coverage, disclosure mechanics, communication choice), not a hand-wave about "market inefficiency."

---

### Section 5 — Risks (2-3 sentences, written as conditionals)

*Purpose:* Each risk is a sentence of the form "if X, then our Y is wrong, and the stock is worth $Z."

*Example:*
> "If [end-market metric] falls below [specific number], [TICKER]'s [year+1] EBITDA growth bridge breaks — pricing alone cannot offset volume, and consensus would be roughly right. If [customer name, who is [%] of revenue per the [date] 10-K] does not renew at its [date] contract expiry, [Y]% of forward EBITDA is at risk. If the [refinance — say it long] of the $[N] million [year] notes is forced at >[X]%, our [year+2] free cash flow estimate falls by $[M]."

*Voice check:* three conditionals, three quantified thresholds, three named documents. A reader can build a watchlist of three datapoints from this paragraph.

---

### Section 6 — What Would Need to Be True (2-3 sentences, the close)

*Purpose:* Buffett's "criteria" template, applied forward. State the three or four things that, if observed, confirm the thesis.

*Example:*
> "Three things would need to be true over the next [N] quarters: (1) [TICKER] reports [specific metric] at or above [number] for [N] consecutive quarters, confirming the disclosure from the [date] 10-K is operational; (2) a peer or customer call references the [structural change] by name, lifting the change from buried disclosure to consensus narrative; (3) [specific catalyst — divestiture closes, contract renewal announced, segment broken out]. Each is observable from public filings or earnings calls; none requires private channel checks. Were a controlling operator-investor to take a position, the playbook is unchanged from the minority case — the value creation comes from the disclosed change being priced, not from operational intervention."

*Voice check:* the close is a list of testable observations, not a value range or a "compelling opportunity" line. The final sentence makes the operator/controlling-owner frame explicit, which is what Lunchline is reading for.

---

### Footer — Sourcing summary (1 line)

A single line at the bottom of the page listing the *named* documents the memo relies on. Not "company filings" — actual document titles with dates.

*Example:* "Sources: FY2024 10-K (filed [date]); Q1 2026 10-Q (filed [date]); Q[N] [year] earnings call transcript; [Peer A] Q[N] [year] earnings call transcript; [Customer / supplier] [year] investor day deck."

### Swap-in notes

- **For Template B (inflection-point arc):** replace Section 2's "what the market sees" with a *historical* description of the company pre-inflection. Make Section 3's variant view the post-inflection identity. The mispricing reason in Section 4 then becomes "the financial statements have not yet caught up."
- **For Template C (market-regime arc):** Section 1 expands to two paragraphs — the company in the *old* regime, then the company in the *new* regime. Section 4's mispricing reason becomes "the multiple reflects the old regime; the cash flows will reflect the new one."

## 8. Checklist (run after the first draft)

Before sending the draft to review, check every sentence against this list. If any sentence fails, rewrite it.

1. Does the first sentence of every section contain a number?
2. Is every number followed (within the same sentence) by a preposition pointing to a named source?
3. Are all terms of art either translated at first use or replaced with plain language?
4. Is there exactly one analogy in the memo, used once?
5. Is there at least one direct quote from management, dated and sourced?
6. Is there at least one peer cross-reference (named peer + named document)?
7. Are the risks written as conditionals with quantified thresholds, not as nouns?
8. Does the close hand judgment to the reader, or does it sell?
9. Could a teenager — with the document titles in hand — verify the three most load-bearing claims in the memo?
10. Have all instances of "compelling," "attractive," "asymmetric," "world-class," "best-in-class," and "high-quality" been deleted?
