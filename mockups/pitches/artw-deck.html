Art's-Way Manufacturing · ARTW 03 / 13 · Business Overview

The building segment serves marquee research institutions from two Iowa plants, with no customer over 10% of sales and a 70-year operating history behind it.

The customer list reads like a who's-who of life-science research; the company has built more than 150 of these facilities since launching the unit in 2006.
Customers
The FY2025 largest customer was about 9% of consolidated revenue, with none over 10%. Named modular clients include UC Davis, MD Anderson, ADM, SAIC, the New Iberia Research Center, Honeywell, the Stowers Institute, and Stanford. Farm-equipment customers are grain and livestock producers reached through the dealer network.
Geography
Two plants, both in Iowa: Armstrong (headquarters and farm equipment, ~249,000 sq ft) and Monona (Art's-Way Scientific modular labs, built 2007, ~50,000 sq ft).
Earnings quality: FY25 profit was a one-time credit
ARTW reported $1.04M of net income in FY25, but $1.51M of that was an Employee Retention Credit, a one-time pandemic-era payroll-tax refund that will not repeat. Strip it out and the company was roughly break-even before tax. The clean earning power is the building segment's $1.75M operating profit, against the farm segment's $1.46M loss.

Art's-Way Manufacturing · ARTW 04 / 13 · End Market

The building segment sells into a fragmented niche of a ~$16.5–25.6B US modular market growing 7–8% a year.

Within that broad market, the two legs sit in their own sub-markets: a ~$4.3B US modular cleanroom market for research labs, and a slice of US agricultural building construction for animal biosecurity. The two draw on separate federal budgets, so a slowdown in one can be offset by the other.

Cleanroom is the addressable market for the research-lab work; broader cleanroom construction is forecast to ~$11.7B by 2030. For animal biosecurity there is no clean published US dollar TAM, so we frame it honestly as a slice of ag building construction, with live demand pulled by USDA's $1B avian-flu program.
Research laboratories (~60%)
Custom $1M–$5M buildings: high-containment BSL-3 labs (rated for dangerous pathogens) and vivariums (buildings for housing research animals). Buyers: NIH-funded universities, drug companies, government public-health labs. Sub-market: ~$4.3B US modular cleanrooms.
Animal-biosecurity buildings (~40%)
Buildings that keep disease out of swine, livestock, and poultry operations. This leg grew ARTW's farm-building sales by $1.355M in FY25. Sub-market: a slice of US ag building construction (~5%/yr).
How federal budgets reach this business
Research side: federal science budgets
NIH, USDA research, and public-health budgets fund the work through ARTW's clients: universities, government labs, and pharma use grant and capital budgets to build the vivariums and containment labs ARTW makes. Caveat: that funding climate is uncertain, but the proposed 15% cap on university research overhead was enjoined (First Circuit, Jan 6, 2026), so it is uncertainty, not an enacted cut.
Animal side: USDA's $1B avian-flu program
$500M is for on-farm biosecurity, cost-shared up to 75%. That money reaches ARTW through farmers who use the cost-share, and through disease-control rules that drive demand for biosecure animal buildings.
The mechanism in both cases: federal money → clients' capital budgets → building purchases. The two budgets are unrelated, so the legs do not move together.

Art's-Way Manufacturing · ARTW 05 / 13 · Competitive Positioning

Art's-Way builds a specialized lab in about six months, against two to five years for conventional construction.

Customers who need a containment lab quickly pay for delivery in roughly six months rather than waiting years for an on-site build. That speed, plus the engineering to meet containment codes, is why the segment earns 17% where general modular builders earn about 7%.
  • 01
    A record of finished, specialized buildings.More than 20 vivariums, more than 15 BSL-3 containment labs, and over 150 buildings delivered, for buyers including UC Davis, MD Anderson, ADM, and the Stowers Institute.
  • 02
    Speed is the real difference.Delivery in about six months versus two to five years for a conventional design-and-build project. That matters most to research labs on a deadline and to farms responding to a live disease outbreak.
  • 03
    Meeting containment codes is hard to copy.The 10-K lists the barriers to entry: containment engineering, trained labor, access to the bidding process, and existing customer relationships. The real biocontainment-modular competitors are CERTEK and Germfree; WillScot and BOXX (formerly Vanguard) are modular-leasing businesses, not custom-lab fabricators.
Operating margin: this segment versus general modular builders
What the margin supports for value
The speed and containment expertise support about 7–9× operating earnings, in line with Fonar Corporation (Nasdaq: FONR), a small, family-controlled, healthcare-adjacent company taken private by its own CEO in December 2025 at roughly 7–9×. It does not earn the 12–14× of a leasing business like WillScot, because it does not collect recurring rent.

Art's-Way Manufacturing · ARTW 06 / 13 · Investment Thesis

This is an operator play: a declining farm-equipment business bolted to a high-value building business, where the price reflects the company as it runs today, not the growth a focused owner could unlock.

The thesis is not that the market has misread a screen. It is that an owner could divest the loss-making farm segment, put capital and focus behind the building segment, and grow it. At $2.58 the stock is priced fairly for the status quo, so the return is one an owner creates.
  • 01
    Farm equipment is a declining, cash-consuming legacy to divest.Sales fell to $12.7M and the segment lost money two years running (−$1.46M in FY25). It ties up $19.2M of assets, mostly aged inventory, and is the source of the company's negative operating cash flow.
  • 02
    The building business is the high-value asset to invest behind and scale.Sales rose every year, $7.8M → $9.8M → $10.2M, at a 17% operating margin on just $3.3M of assets, and backlog was up 103% as of February 2026. It deserves capital and focus the conglomerate denies it.
  • 03
    Today's price reflects the status quo, not the growth a focused owner could create.At about book value, the price assumes the business as run today. Assets also protect the downside: current ratio 2.30×, long-term debt $2.73M below working capital $8.34M (both safety tests pass), net current assets ~$1.08/share.

Art's-Way Manufacturing · ARTW 07 / 13 · Investment Thesis (cont.)

The building business is priced about fairly today, so the return is one an owner creates by improving it, not by buying it cheaply.

Subtract a realistic value for the farm assets from the $19.8M enterprise value, and the building business is left at about 7.8 times operating earnings, roughly fair. That is the honest center of the thesis.

Fairly priced, not free
On a realistic farm-asset value, the building business is already priced at about 6 to 9 times operating earnings, roughly 7.8× in the base case. It does not come free: the company carries $6.4M of net debt and a large farm-inventory balance that recovers only at a discount.
Where the return comes from
From an owner, not a cheap entry price: divest the farm segment, run the building business on its own, and grow it. The next slides lay out those steps and what they could be worth.
The catalyst is family-gated
None of it happens unless the McConnell family agrees, and no plan has been announced. The path exists: FONR shows a family take-private clears at ~7–9× plus a ~31% premium. But the timing is not in an outside investor's hands.

Art's-Way Manufacturing · ARTW 09 / 13 · Value Creation Plan

An owner could roughly double the building segment's value through four levers, one in each of the case's four categories.

On its own today the building business is worth about $12.1M; if the four levers all work, about $22.6M. The bars break down the $10.5M gap. Allocations are estimated; endpoints and total match the valuation model.
Commercial
Professionalize sales
Backlog more than doubled on ~$97,000 of advertising; the constraint is sales effort, not demand. The president went part-time and the sales director was not replaced. Add a business-development manager.
Operations
Add recurring monitoring
Offer ongoing containment monitoring (the connected sensors these labs must keep recertified) and add fabrication capacity. Recurring service revenue earns a higher multiple than one-time project work.
Capital structure
Divest farm, retire debt
Wind down the farm segment, sell ~$8–10M of inventory and the plant, retire the revolver. That removes the cash drain and lets the building business re-rate as a clean platform.
Mergers & acquisitions
Roll up the niche
The fragmented biocontainment niche (CERTEK, Germfree) is rollable. ARTW has only ever divested, never bought within modular, an untapped lever to add scale and service revenue.

On base-case assumptions the deal roughly breaks even; the return depends on the optimistic case, so an owner must believe the plan before paying a control premium.

A controlling stake costs a premium over market. At the 31.5% premium paid for FONR, the buy-in is $3.39 per share. The bars show MOIC, the multiple of invested capital, how many times the money comes back. Below 1.0× is a loss.

What the chart says
At a normal control premium, the base case returns less than the money put in. The deal works only in the optimistic case, where the building business converts the doubled backlog into sales, reaches about 10× earnings as a focused business, and starts acquiring small competitors.
The single fact that decides it
The split between the two legs and how durable the growth is. If research-lab demand holds and farm-biosecurity orders repeat, the optimistic case is live. If the 49% jump was a one-time response to bird flu and research stays cautious, the base case of $2.46, below the price, is the ceiling.

Tools used

  • Claude Code (Claude Opus): screening, filing extraction, the valuation build, and this deck.

Where AI helped

  • Scanning a wide universe of small companies to surface Art's-Way for closer study.
  • Rebuilding the segment history, the one-time credit, and the cash-flow detail directly from the annual report.
  • Running a build-then-review loop that moved the conclusion from a hopeful "buy it cheap" read to the sober "fairly priced, the return is operational" answer in this deck.

Where review corrected the AI

  • Caught a number tuned to the price. An early draft valued the farm assets generously enough to land fair value at exactly the $2.58 price. Review reset that to a defensible recovery, moving the base case to $2.46, slightly below the price.
  • Corrected the policy claim to the filings. A claimed "enacted 40% research cut" was wrong: the proposed 15% cap on research overhead was enjoined, and the injunction upheld by the First Circuit in January 2026. The climate is cautious, not a confirmed cut.
  • Flagged the estimate openly. The 60/40 split between the two legs is inferred from one disclosed figure and labeled as the key open question.

What the filings cannot settle

  • The split between the two legs, the age of the farm inventory, and the family's intentions are not disclosed. Each would require primary research, such as management calls or customer checks.
  • Comparable-company values and the current share price should be re-checked against a live source before this is published.
